How it works

One formula, four chasers

M  =  163  −  Brewers wins  −  chaser losses
  • Each race measures the Brewers against a different chaser — which is why a Cubs loss moves the division number and nothing else.
  • A Brewers win drops all four numbers by one. It is the only result that does.
  • A Brewers loss moves nothing. Losses don't raise a magic number — they only burn a game off the schedule.
  • The 163 is 162 games plus one, because the extra game is what turns a tie into a clinch. MLB now breaks ties mathematically, so where the Brewers own the head-to-head tiebreaker the real number can be one lower.
  • If the chaser changes overnight, a number can rise even after a win. The feed says so when it happens.

Method

What goes into a projection

  • Recent form — last 15 games, blended at 30% against a 45% season baseline. Fifteen games is roughly 60 plate appearances, too noisy to trust on its own.
  • Platoon split — performance against the handedness of tonight’s probable starter, at 25%.
  • Starter and bullpen — opponent batting average for the starter and for the rested relievers likely to appear, blended by expected exposure.
  • Park and weather — temperature and wind, applied only to extra-base outcomes. A closed roof zeroes this out entirely.
  • Lineup slot — leadoff hitters get a fifth trip far more often than the bottom third, which moves a total-bases line more than most matchup factors.

Legs are priced by convolving the per-plate-appearance outcome distribution, not by a normal approximation — across 3–5 trips the distribution is lumpy, and “2+ total bases” depends on exactly that.

Read this before betting

The model is a projection, not a prediction, and it can be confidently wrong. Parlay legs from the same game are correlated — they share a pitcher, a park and a game state — so the combined probability shown is shaded down but still optimistic. Every added leg multiplies the house edge along with the payout. Bet only what you would shrug off losing.
